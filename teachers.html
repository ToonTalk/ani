Ken Kahn's Ani (MIT, 1978), running again

A guide for teachers and parents

On Ani's Cinderella, learners watch a film made in 1978 by an AI program, ask any character why it does what it does, change the story, teach Ani and criticize its films, and compare Ani's way of thinking with today's language models. This guide has session plans, printable activity cards, discussion questions and practical notes for classrooms, libraries and university courses, and, for a parent or grandparent at home with a child, Young children.

At a glance

Ani is the program in Ken Kahn's MIT PhD thesis (1979), which he rewrote substantially as the technical report AI-TR-540. Given a short description of a story, it reasons its way to an animated film of moving shapes, and records why it made every choice. The film page runs Ken's 1978 program again, in the browser, so learners can question it, change the story and see what Ani does.

Ages
6 and up with an adult nearby; 13 and up on their own; university. The page has an age setting for 6 to 11 (below).
Time
One 45 to 60 minute workshop, or 90 minutes, or three shorter sessions.
Equipment
Laptops or desktops with Chrome, one for every two or three learners; a projector helps.

What students will learn

A piece of AI history

How AI was done at the MIT AI Lab in the 1970s: knowledge written by hand, reasoning with symbols, programs built from “actors” that send each other messages. The page runs Ani's actual 1978 code, recovered from the lab's backup tapes, on a MacLisp interpreter rebuilt for the revival: not an imitation of it.

Two kinds of AI, side by side

Ani reasons from knowledge Ken wrote by hand; a language model writes from patterns learned from vast amounts of text. On this page they work together (a model can turn a story into Ani's notation, and Ani does the rest), so students meet both in one task and can see what each is good and bad at.

How a program can explain itself

Ken called Ani a “glass box”. Every choice comes with its reasons: the chain from an action up to what the story asked for, the alternatives weighed and their scores, the suggestions kept and set aside, down to the line of code. Students learn what makes an explanation checkable, and how that differs from a model explaining itself after the fact.

Debugging and testing

Predict, run, compare. Change one word and see what changes; find where Ani stops on a new story, read why, and see what the revival's repairs do about it. These are the habits of anyone who builds or tests software, and of scientists running experiments.

Narrative and character

Ani's characters never speak. Personality, feelings and relationships are shown only by how shapes move: fast or slow, straight or curvy, toward someone or away. Students see what a story needs to say, and what a storyteller (or a program) has to invent.

Ages and what they need

No programming is needed. Knowing the Cinderella story helps. At 12+, the page's setting for adults and older students, some of the text is dense: the first line or two of each panel are the important part, and the rest is there on a click. For younger learners, set the page's age (below).

Using Ani with young children

The film page has an age setting, Ages: 6–11 · 12+, at the top. It changes how the page presents Ani, never what Ani does: every film, and Ken's 1978 film, is the same at either setting, and the setting is never stored in a film, a film file, a criticism or a notebook. (Until 21 October 2026 there were three settings, 6–8, 9–11 and 12+; Ken's grandchildren of 5 and 7 had no trouble with the 9–11 wording, so it became 6–11. Old links to 6–8 or 9–11 open 6–11.)

SettingWhat changes
12+Nothing: the page as it has always been. It is the default, so existing links and visitors see what they saw before. The one addition is a small Speech beside the age control, where Read aloud and Talk to Ani can be turned on; both are off until someone does.
6–11Everything the page shows is said in everyday words: not “the revival's extensions” but “the new parts added to Ani in 2026”, not “a language model” but “an AI”, not “Ken's notation” but “words Ani can read”, not “criticism” but “fixes”, and the scenes as what Ani was asked to show; the full list is in the project's source (reports/chapter-x/ages-6-11-words.md). Captions, a character's panel and What Ani was told are said in shorter sentences. They are filled in from the same records as the full sentences, by fixed templates, never by a language model, so they can only say what Ani's record says. Ani's own words stay as Ani has them; a few neutral ones (dominates, is tolerant of, hard working, …) have a dotted underline and open a short meaning on a click. Who decided is a three-step ladder: The story says…, Ani knows…, So Ani chose…. Each panel ends in More about Ani's choices, the full 12+ panel of the same moment, with the code citations and the Lisp. Seconds replace frame numbers; frames a second and TV or Color move under Film settings; Tell Ani a new story folds its model settings under More settings; Teach Ani opens on its two simplest kinds; Read aloud is offered, off until pressed. The characters are buttons under the film that open each one's panel. Bigger, beside the film's controls, makes the type and every control larger (Play, Back and Next, the scenes), for younger children or a touch screen. The header links to the guide for students and to this part of this guide.
  • Set it with a link. Add #ages-6-11 or #ages-12 to the page's address: index.html#ages-6-11. It combines with a link to a moment, such as index.html#kept-apart.48.ages-6-11 (Kept Apart, at its frame 48 at 4 a second). The page says once that the link set it, and the setting is kept in that browser until someone changes it.
  • Read aloud uses the computer's own voices, through the browser: nothing is sent by the page, and the computer's local voices work without the internet. It reads each new caption as the film plays, and any box's Read this. The voice says Ani as “Annie”. In Speech settings (under the film at 6–11; at 12+ the small Speech beside the age control, where Read aloud is off until turned on there, and then appears beside Play too) a grown-up chooses the voice and how fast it speaks, and, at 6–11, whether the film waits for the voice before it moves on, which suits a young child (at 12+ it always keeps going). Someone using a screen reader may prefer Read aloud off.
  • Talk to Ani is off until a grown-up turns it on, on each computer, in Speech settings; a link can't turn it on. It uses the browser's speech recognition, which in Chrome sends what is said to Google to turn it into words (in Edge to Microsoft, in Safari to Apple); the switch says so. Then a microphone appears beside the boxes (a new story, Tell Ani what's wrong, a reason in Teach Ani): the words go into the box, and nothing is sent to Ani or a model until someone presses the box's own button. At 6–11 a microphone beside Play also takes a few spoken commands: “play”, “stop”, “why”, “next scene”, “go back”, “read it again”. Firefox, the claude.ai copy and a computer without the internet have no recognition; there the settings point to the computer's own dictation (Windows+H on Windows, the Fn key twice on a Mac, the keyboard's microphone on an iPad), which types into the page's boxes.

Five short activities, with a grown-up

At 6–11, each about 5 to 10 minutes, for a child of 6 to 9 with a grown-up. Let the child press Play and tap; the grown-up reads, or lets Read aloud read, and asks the question.

Y1

Meet the shapes

Ages 6–9 · 5 minutes
Do

Play The Introduction without stopping. Then tap each character's button under the film, one at a time.

Say it together

Who do you think is in charge? How can you tell, when nobody says a word?

Y2

Why does the stepmother do that?

Ages 6–9 · 10 minutes
Do

Pause while the stepmother (the square) is pushing Cinderella (the star), and tap the stepmother. Listen to the first sentence, then open Who decided.

Say it together

Did the story Ani was told say “push”? So who thought of it? (The grown-up can check in What Ani was told: it didn't. Ani chose pushing to show how the two characters feel about each other.)

Y3

Nobody moves!

Ages 6–9 · 10 minutes
Do

Play the end of The Meeting, where Cinderella and the Prince stop for several seconds. Tap one of them.

Say it together

They are very happy, but why can't we see it? What would you have them do? (Ani shows a feeling by how a character moves, so with nowhere to go, nothing shows.)

Y4

Make it last longer

Ages 6–9 · 10 minutes, a few of them waiting
Do

The grown-up first sets 4 a second in Film settings, under the film, so the remake is quick. Watch No Longer Kept Apart, which is over in a moment. Under Fixing this film, choose No Longer Kept Apart is too quick, then Make it again with these fixes, and watch the two side by side.

Say it together

Is it better now? Ken thought the scene was too quick in 1979, too.

Y5

Tell Ani your story

Ages 6–9 · 20 minutes · needs a language model
Do

The child tells a short story of two or three characters who like or dislike each other; the grown-up types it into Tell Ani a new story, or the child says it with Talk to Ani if a grown-up has turned it on. Use Gemini Nano where the computer has it (nothing leaves the computer), write the description, look it over together, and press Make it quickly.

Say it together

Is that your story? What did Ani change, and why?

Younger children use the page with a grown-up, and Change the story's editor is much the same at either setting: it is for the grown-up or an older child. We suggest the grown-up also handles Tell Ani what's wrong, which sends the words typed to a language model. The design for younger viewers is in the project's source on GitHub.

On a shared computer. What a child teaches Ani is kept on that computer, in that browser. Teach Ani has Save this teaching to a file and Open saved teaching…, so a child can take their teaching away and bring it back, and Forget all teaching saved on this computer, which lists what it will delete and asks first (it is also in What this page keeps in this browser, at the foot of the page). The page's labels don't say “my” or “your” for what is kept there, since it may be the last child's.

Before the session

  • Chrome on a laptop or desktop. Open the film page on each computer before learners arrive. Phones can watch the film and ask why; making films is best on a computer.
  • Make one film yourself. Change the story, A kind stepmother, Make it quickly: at 4 frames a second, a little jerky, it takes about three minutes on a recent computer (about five with Ken's beautified scene). Make it smoothly, at 24, takes about a quarter of an hour (half an hour with the beautified scene). Keep the tab in front: Chrome slows tabs in the background.
  • Set the age, if your learners are under 12, with a link such as index.html#ages-6-11 (Young children). If you want Talk to Ani, turn it on yourself on each computer.
  • Decide whether to use a language model. You don't need one: everything except Tell Ani a new story and Tell Ani what's wrong works without. If you do, choose from the table below and try it beforehand.
  • Print the activity cards (button below), or share the guide for students.

Keys in a classroom

An API key works like a password, and whoever has it can spend money on its owner's account.

  • Use your own key, with a spending limit set at the provider.
  • Type it yourself, on a computer you control. Never have students type real keys, and never show a key on a screen, a slide or a board.
  • On shared computers, leave Remember keys in this browser unticked. The key is then kept only in that tab, and is gone when the tab closes.
  • Run Tell Ani a new story as a whole-group activity: the class writes the story, you press the button.
  • If anyone else may have seen the key, delete it at the provider afterwards and make a new one.
Using the claude.ai copy

The site has a copy of the page made to run inside claude.ai: ani-cinderella-claude-artifact.html. Upload it to a claude.ai chat and ask Claude to copy it, exactly as it is, into an HTML artifact. There, Tell Ani a new story offers Claude on the viewer's own claude.ai account, with no key (claude.ai asks the first time), and Gemini Nano.

The copy is compressed to fit claude.ai's size limit, so its code is hard to read; a note at its top points to the site, toontalk.github.io/ani, and to the readable source, github.com/ToonTalk/ani. It makes the same films as the site's page. Read aloud works there; Talk to Ani doesn't, since claude.ai gives the page no microphone.

claude.ai accounts are for adults, so this is not the route for children's workshops.

Poor Wi-Fi, or none

The page carries everything it needs. Once it has loaded, watching the film, asking why and making films all happen in the browser, with no internet. You can also save the page (Ctrl+S, “Webpage, HTML only”) and open the saved file later; save Ani's Choice Points and Ani's Code in the same folder if you want their links to work. Without the internet the page uses plain fonts, and only Gemini Nano, once downloaded, can tell new stories.

A university seminar one or two meetings

  • Before: read the introduction of AI-TR-540 (pp. 11–23, including Ken's walk through the choice of the stepmother's speed) and its discussion of creativity (pp. 205–212).
  • In class: cards 3, 9, 10 and 13, then hard challenges A and C in pairs; with a second meeting, Section D of AI-TR-540, “Critics and Criticism” (pp. 189–191), then cards 21 and 22 and hard challenge I.
  • Discuss: symbolic AI and language models; what counts as an explanation; who is the author of a generated film.
  • After: a short essay, or a proposal for one piece of knowledge Ani lacks (see Assessment), taught to Ani in Teach Ani and tried on two stories (hard challenge G).

Activity cards

Each card says what students do, what they should find, and what to watch for. The students' guide has the same activities in learners' words.

To print the whole guide, use your browser's Print.
1

Meet the cast

Start here · 5 minutes · any computer
Students do

Watch The Introduction without reading anything, then describe each character from how it moves.

They find

The square (the stepmother) rushes at the star (Cinderella) and shoves it, five times, then chases and hits it; at the end the star and the triangle (the Prince) travel together. Personality and relationships come across without a word.

Watch for

Students reading the side panel first. Ask them to just watch.

2

What was Ani told?

Start here · 5 minutes
Students do

Read What Ani was told, then Show it as Ani read it. Name three things the film shows that the description doesn't mention.

They find

The description has the characters' words, how they feel about each other, and five scenes, each with tasks and a mood, rhythm and length. It never says where anyone stands, how fast they go or what they do: Ani decides all of that.

Watch for

The Lisp can put younger learners off. It's optional.

3

Ask the stepmother why

Start here · 10 minutes
Students do

Pause The Introduction while the square pushes the star, click the square and open Why.

They find

The chain of reasons: she pushes Cinderella around to establish that she dominates and hates her, as the film description asks. The word “push” is nowhere in the description: pushing is Ani's way of showing that relationship. Why so long: the push repeats five times because Ken's code for it says so, and the scene is meant to be long, 30 to 75 seconds.

Watch for

Clicks that miss the shape. The ◂ and ▸ buttons step one frame at a time.

4

A moment when no one moves

Start here · 10 minutes
Students do

Find the stillness near the end of The Meeting, at 24 a second, or the slanted no one moves line in The Meeting, part by part. Click it, and open Why no one moves.

They find

About six seconds where nothing moves. The last part is only feelings (Cinderella and the Prince are very happy); Ani shows feelings by changing how characters move, but no one is moving, so nothing shows. AI-TR-540 says such changes cannot stand alone (p. 125). With the revival's extensions (SEEN), the two wander about instead. In the strip under the film that part is the thin orange bar: a part of only feelings, which Ken's SCENE runs alongside the part before it, made longer to hold it (scene.9:216-240); the strip's key and each part's hover title say so.

Watch for

At 4 a second the pause is under two seconds and easy to miss: use 24.

5

Make the stepmother kind

Start here · 15 minutes, 2 to 3 of them waiting
Students do

Write down three predictions. Change the story, A kind stepmother, Ani as in 1978 (Ken's program, unchanged) (so that the change is the only difference from Ken's film), Make it quickly; then watch side by side.

They find

Made by Ani as in 1978, 21 of the 102 choices of how the characters move come out differently (the stepmother is slower and less deliberate, and now likes others). In The Introduction she no longer pushes and hits Cinderella; she goes to her and keeps her company. But in Kept Apart she still stands guard: the scene's tasks, which weren't changed, say she prevents the meeting. Character words change how characters move; the plot lives in the scenes. (At 4 a second, the fairy godmother also stands guard in No Longer Kept Apart, keeping the stepmother away.)

Watch for

Tabs left in the background: Chrome slows them. Give waiting students card 4 or 6 to do.

6

Find a word Ani doesn't know

Start here · 10 minutes
Students do

Open Change the story and read the notes under the word lists before changing anything.

They find

Ken's own story has the fairy godmother is protective of Cinderella; Ani knows only “is protective to”, so the word is remembered but suggests nothing. And “strong”, a word for personality, is given as the Prince's looks. Neither stopped Ani in 1978. Below the characters, each scene has a card (its tasks, which can be taken out or added to, only with tasks Ani can plan, and menus for its mood, rhythm and length), and the whole film has its length and its levels, each saying what it changes in Ani's choices, with Ken's code; where Ani as in 1978 reads nothing (a scene's mood and rhythm, the film's rhythm level and mood), the page says so. Edit the whole film description as text opens the Lisp, which the menus follow.

Watch for

A good bridge to discussion: should a program stop when it meets something it doesn't understand, or carry on quietly?

7

Why is the Prince in the first scene?

Going further · 10 minutes
Students do

Click the triangle at the end of The Introduction (the description of that scene names only Cinderella and the stepmother), then Cinderella, and open Why.

They find

Cinderella goes to the Prince and goes along with him to establish what Cinderella is like. To show a trait, Ani may need someone to show it with, and brings them in.

Watch for

A chance to talk about inference: Ani adds what the description implies but never says.

8

Tell Ani a new story

Going further · 20 minutes · needs a language model
Students do

Tell Ani a new story; a ready-made story (Red Riding Hood, The Tortoise and the Hare, Beauty and the Beast) or their own; Write the film description; read the result before making the film.

They find

The description in plain words; notes where the page tidied it; any problems Ani's checks found, with a button to ask the model to fix them; and differences from the story they told. Ani can show only what characters are like, how they feel and what they do to each other, so talking, objects and places have to become chases, guarding, rescues and feelings. Once the film is made, the words told stay editable in What Ani was told (also for a film opened again from Films made here): Change the story and make it again has the model write the description again, shown with Ani's checks before Ani makes the film.

Watch for

Models inventing characters, or making a prize or a place into a character. That is worth discussing, not just fixing. Simple stories of a few characters who love, hate, chase, guard and rescue work best.

9

Where Ani stops, and why

Going further · 15 minutes
Students do

Make a new story with Ani as in 1978 (Ken's program, unchanged) chosen (new films are made with the revival's extensions until it is). If it stops, read Where Ani as in 1978 stopped and What Lisp said; then Make it again with the revival's extensions.

They find

A plain-English reason for each stop, with the 1978 program's own message. For example, a tortoise-and-hare story stopped because nothing suggested how active the hare should be, so pass 1 gave up on it and pass 2 couldn't go on. Made again, the side panel says what each repair did.

Watch for

Students treating a stop as the page breaking. It is Ani as Ken left it, meeting a story he never gave it.

10

Ani as in 1978, or with the revival's extensions

Going further · 15 minutes, about 3 of them waiting
Students do

Make Ken's story, unchanged, with Ani with the revival's extensions, the page's setting unless Ani as in 1978 was chosen (Make it quickly); read Which extensions; compare with Ken's film.

They find

None of the 102 choices of how they move change, but Ani now draws the characters itself (at the time of writing, Cinderella as a small purple hexagon and the Prince as a large red circle, for example) and says why. The Introduction gains new parts (Cinderella helps the Prince along, and follows her stepmother), and at the end of The Meeting the two wander instead of standing still.

Watch for

The question underneath: if new code is written in Ken's style from his own ideas, whose program is it?

11

The scene Ken left out

Going further · 15 minutes, about 5 of them waiting
Students do

Change the story, Cinderella beautified, make the film; watch the new scene; click Cinderella in the scenes after it.

They find

Ken wrote this scene in 1978 and left it commented out. Made with the extensions, the fairy godmother goes to Cinderella and transforms her at once (she is magical: the revival's rule); then Ani takes a fresh look at Cinderella as if the character were brand new (AI-TR-540, p. 168), and the side panel says how she has changed.

Watch for

It needs the extensions, which new films are made with; if Ani as in 1978 was chosen, the page switches back to them for this scene.

12

Two films of one story

Going further · 10 minutes
Students do

Watch a scene at 24 a second, then at 4, and find differences; then compare a film of their own with Ken's, side by side (Compare two films, over the screen, puts any two films kept in the browser, or Ken's, side by side, with what changed between them).

They find

The two rates are two different runs: even The Introduction is filmed differently (about 38 seconds at 24, about 48 at 4), and each later scene is planned from where the last one's filming left the characters. 24 is the rate of Ken's camera; 4, of his TV previews and the flipbook printed in AI-TR-540.

Watch for

A good moment to ask what “the” film is, when one program and one story give two.

Note

Over the screen, the first button is Ani's 1978 film until a film of a new story is made; then it is the film made before that one, by its name, so Side by side compares the newest two. A changed Cinderella keeps Ken's film there. Whatever two films a student chooses with Compare two films, or on the left and on the right in Films made here, stay chosen, after a reload too.

13

Inside a choice point

Going further · 15 minutes
Students do

Open Ani's Choice Points, read the stepmother's speed (As the thesis tells it), then explore others.

They find

The stepmother's speed (high) was decided at attempt 825 of 836, after 10 postponements. The four characters' speeds wait on one another until the film's focus breaks the deadlock. Ken walks through this choice on pp. 20–23 of AI-TR-540.

Watch for

The table is dense. Point students at one cell at a time.

14

Teach Ani “grumpy”

Start here · Teach Ani · 15 minutes, about 3 of them waiting
Students do

In Teach Ani, Try teaching, grumpy is like mean: make Ken's story with the stepmother grumpy, a word Ani doesn't know, then again with the knowledge that grumpy means much the same as mean.

They find

Not knowing the word, Ani picks her attraction to enemies from the film's variety, so she is drawn to Cinderella and chases her before hitting her (the Introduction, 226 frames). With the knowledge, the film is exactly Ken's with “mean” (225 frames, both with MEND and KNOW), and her glass box says “she is grumpy, which you taught Ani means much the same as mean”.

Watch for

Students expecting “nothing” the first time: an unknown word isn't ignored, it leaves a gap that something else fills.

15

A slow grumpy stepmother

Start here · Teach Ani · 15 minutes, about 3 of them waiting
Students do

Predict her speed, then make grumpy moves slowly (grumpy: speed low, medium strongly).

They find

She moves at medium speed: her other word, strong, says fast, and Ani met the two in the middle. Only 1 of the 102 choices changed; her walk to hit Cinderella takes 12.5 seconds instead of 7.3 (the Introduction, 234 frames). What the teaching did calls the piece Combined.

Watch for

A good moment for “knowledge interacts”: one new fact rarely acts alone.

16

A word of your own

Start here · Teach Ani · 20 minutes
Students do

Tell Ani what's wrong, in your own words

Going further · Criticize the film · 20 minutes · needs a language model
Students do

Under Criticism of this film, open Tell Ani what's wrong, write what is wrong in plain English, and press Ask … to read it; then decide, item by item, what to do.

They find

The model reads their words under a strict schema whose choices are this film's own scenes, parts, ways of showing, characters and Ani's words, so it can't name something the film doesn't have. Each item has its own button, and nothing is applied until it is pressed: criticism Ani can heed goes to Criticism of this film; a change to the story opens Change the story with the change made there to look over (a tortoise-and-hare story's the hare is unhappy and should move slowly in the final scene becomes the hare's feeling in that scene); knowledge true of any character opens Teach Ani, its form filled in; and what Ani has no notion of (mood, humor, rhythm) is refused with Ken's words on p. 191.

Watch for

The sorting is the lesson: criticism of how a film was made, a change to what it is to show, and knowledge. The words, with the film as the glass box tells it, go only to the provider chosen; run it on the teacher's computer with a key, or with Gemini Nano.

A one-minute Cinderella

Students do

Director keeps to Ani's times

They find

A–J

The hard challenges

Hard · 20 to 45 minutes each · keen students, older students
Why hard

Each depends on what Ani actually does, which is found only by running it, so a frontier AI model asked the same question usually gets it wrong too. Have students record a prediction before they check, and let them compare a chatbot's predictions with theirs.

  • A. Predict before you look (a shy Prince): score their predictions of the Prince's nine ways of moving against How Ani's choices changed.
  • B. Make it happen: change words until the film meets a target (the Prince slower than Cinderella; a mean stepmother who never touches Cinderella in The Introduction). Some targets may be impossible; accept a well-evidenced argument that one is.
  • C. Explain a curve: a chain of “because”s from Ken's words to Cinderella's curving path. Expect: high curvature, suggested because she is graceful and good (the opposite of evil) and by the film's variety and energy; a “low” from the film's low flashiness was set aside. The stepmother's path curves little.
  • D. A part that takes no time: part 4 of The Introduction, “Cinderella helps the Prince”, lasts 0 seconds, because the 1978 Ani had no way to show helping; with the extensions (KNOW) it has one.
  • E. A story the 1978 Ani can film: two or three characters, at least two scenes, no stop. It usually takes several tries; the notes on each stop are the learning.
  • F. Predict the 102: before making a film with a piece of their knowledge, predict which of Ani's 102 choices it changes, and score against What the teaching did; then against a chatbot's predictions.
  • G. Ken's rule: knowledge that keeps the mean stepmother from touching Cinderella in The Introduction, yet is true of any character with her words in any story; then try it on another story. Was it knowledge, or a criticism of one film?
  • H. Two notebooks: swap notebook files with a partner, make the same story with each, and explain every difference from the side panel alone.
  • I. Not until the third scene: Ken's own test case for criticism (AI-TR-540, p. 64), keeping Cinderella and the Prince apart in the first two scenes; predict what Ani picks instead to establish her personality, and whether they stay apart. Expect: with Ken's knowledge Ani picks helping the Prince, which it has no way to show (challenge D), so the Introduction shrinks to 151 frames; the plan keeps them apart, but in Kept Apart's film they still come very close, and the page says the criticism was only partly met.
  • J. Which scene runs long? Ken's own film at 24 a second is close to its plan overall (Asked for 2 to 5 minutes; planned 139 s; filmed 135 s — how), but The Meeting runs about 11 seconds past its plan (its last part, the two joys at high speed, which Director takes four times as long over as Ani estimated) and Justice ends about 11 short (the stepmother gets near the couple sooner than estimated). Students predict which, then check with how under the strip.

Discussion questions

Each opens on a click to what to listen for.

Ani explains every choice. Can a language model?

Listen for the difference between a record of the reasons a program actually used and a plausible explanation written afterward. Ani's reasons can be checked against its code; a model's explanation of itself is more text it generated. But Ani's explanations are only as good as Ken's rules, and a model can explain many things Ani never could.

What does it mean that Ani can't show winning?

The page tells the language model that Ani can't show places, objects, races, winning or speech, so a win becomes the winner's joy and pride and the loser's shame. AI-TR-540 says Ani cannot handle very concrete expressions such as “a glass slipper” (p. 19). Listen for: Ani knows characters, feelings, relationships and movement, nothing else; winning needs a race, rules and a finish line. What would Ani need to know to show it?

Who made the Cinderella film: Ani, or Ken?

Ken asked it himself: who really made the Cinderella film — Ani or me? (p. 207). His answer is that it is a matter of degree: how many levels of reasoning, how many choices, how much knowledge. Extend it: who made a film from a student's story, told by a language model, planned by Ani, with repairs written in 2026?

Is Ani creative?

Ken lists the objections, among them Ani only does what it's told, humans only sometimes do (p. 209), and then argues against them: Ani has rules for when to follow, change or reject its own suggestions, and breaks them often (pp. 210–211). Students can find examples in the glass box: suggestions set aside.

The kind stepmother still keeps Cinderella from the Prince. Bug, or feature?

Listen for the idea that the description has two parts, the characters and the scenes, and that changing one doesn't rewrite the other. Is a stepmother who is kind but still stands guard a contradiction, or a more interesting character?

Ani stops when it meets something it can't handle; a language model always writes something. Which would you rather have?

Listen for: it depends what's at stake. A stop is honest but unhelpful; a confident answer is helpful but can be wrong. Ani also carries on quietly in places (the word it doesn't know in Ken's own story): is that better or worse than stopping?

Is a film made with the revival's extensions still Ken's Ani's film?

The extensions are new Lisp, written in Ken's style from ideas in AI-TR-540, in files of their own. Ken's 1978 film is always shown as it was made. Is a repaired Ani the same program? (A version of the Ship of Theseus.)

Ani takes some criticism and refuses other kinds. What can't you criticize, and why?

Ken wrote that Ani could not respond to criticisms of the film's rhythm, nor of climax, tension, mood, or humor, because any aspect of Ani that is built-in … or lacking entirely cannot be criticized with any effect (p. 191). Listen for: criticism works only where the program made a choice it can make differently. Then compare a language model, which will accept any criticism and write something new: is that better?

Criticism, a change to the story, or knowledge?

“The fairy godmother is gone too quickly” is criticism of how Ani made one film; “the hare should be unhappy at the end” changes what the film is to show; “grumpy characters move slowly” is knowledge, true in any story. Tell Ani what's wrong sorts a learner's words into these three. Listen for whether students can say which is which, and why it matters who decides each.

Ani's knowledge was written by one person. A language model's comes from millions. What follows?

Listen for bias (whose ideas of “evil” or “graceful”?), for mistakes and who can find and fix them, and for who is responsible for what the program does.

Assessment ideas

  • Prediction log. Predict, run, score, and explain one prediction that was wrong, using the glass box.
  • Explain a choice. A paragraph explaining one of Ani's choices with evidence: the chain under Why, the suggestions kept and set aside, the code it points to.
  • Bug report. A story where Ani as in 1978 stopped: what happened, where, why, and what the repair did.
  • Design proposal. One piece of knowledge Ani lacks (a way to show winning, say): which words, what they would suggest, and how it would look in movement.
  • Read the notebooks. The reasons students give with each piece they teach Ani (because) are the explicit knowledge Ken's 1977 paper hoped children would come to: collect the notebooks (each can be saved as a file) and read them.
  • A criticism and its outcome. One criticism of a film, what the student expected, and what What the criticism changed says Ani did, met or not, with the reason.
  • Compare explanations. Ask a chatbot why the stepmother pushes Cinderella, then compare its answer with Ani's glass box. Which can be checked, and how?

Troubleshooting

A film is taking a long time

Keep its tab in front: Chrome slows tabs in the background, and the status line above the film says when Chrome has paused it. Make it quickly (4 frames a second) takes about three minutes on a recent computer, about five with Ken's beautified scene; Make it smoothly (24) about a quarter of an hour, half an hour with the beautified scene. A remake with criticism or with taught knowledge is made at the film's own frames a second, so remake Ken's film at 4. Each scene plays as soon as it is filmed. Stop, beside the status line, ends the run.

The film is longer (or shorter) than the length asked for

Ani plans each scene's time from the length asked for, but Director makes each move as it is written, so the film can differ from the plan; the line under the strip says by how much, and why says which moves ran past. To have Director keep to Ani's times, tick Director keeps to Ani's times in Which extensions and make the film again (card 25).

Ani stopped

With Ani as in 1978 chosen, that is Ani as Ken left it, meeting a story he never gave it. The side panel says where and why, in plain words, with what Lisp said a click away. Make it again with the revival's extensions usually gets further. With the extensions, which new films are made with unless Ani as in 1978 is chosen, a stop is one the revival hasn't repaired yet; the side panel says why in the same way. Stops make good discussion (card 9).

The model invented characters, or changed the story

Read the plain-words summary before making the film. Ask the model to fix the problems the page lists, try a stronger model, or open the description in the editor (Look it over, then make the film) and change it there; changes already made in the editor are not lost without a question, and Bring back the text you had undoes the replacement. Small models such as Gemini Nano write rougher drafts. Short, simple stories work best.

Gemini Nano isn't offered, or is downloading

It is built into recent desktop Chrome, on computers that can run it, and downloads the first time it is used (a few gigabytes). If it isn't offered, that computer or browser can't run it; use a key on the teacher's computer instead.

The key doesn't work

Check that the key is for the provider chosen (Claude needs an Anthropic key, ChatGPT an OpenAI key, Gemini a Google AI key), that the account has credit, and that the model chosen is available to it. The page shows the provider's message.

Read aloud says nothing, or there is no microphone

Read aloud and Talk to Ani are offered at either setting: at 6–11 Read aloud is beside Play, off until pressed, and at 12+ both are under the small Speech beside the age control, off until turned on. Read aloud needs a voice on the computer (the speech settings list them, with Try), and browsers speak only after a click on the page, so press Play once. A microphone appears only after a grown-up turns on Talk to Ani in the speech settings, and only in browsers that can turn speech into words (Chrome, Edge, Safari; not Firefox or the claude.ai copy); otherwise use the computer's own dictation. If the browser says the microphone is blocked, allow it in the browser's site settings.

The page is in short sentences and everyday words

Someone set the age to 6–11, here or with a link, and the browser kept it. Press 12+ at the top, or open the page with #ages-12.

The characters look different in a new film

New films are made with the revival's extensions unless Ani as in 1978 (Ken's program, unchanged) is chosen, under the Make buttons. With them, Ani chooses each character's shape, size and colors itself (APPEAR), and the side panel says why. Ken's 1978 film is always drawn as Ken drew it.

A film made earlier has gone

The page keeps films in the browser that made them. A private or guest window, a different computer, or clearing the browser's data loses them. To keep a film, use Save: a film file opens on the page again later, with all of Ani's reasons; a video plays anywhere.

Safety and privacy

What the page keeps. In the browser on that computer only: settings (the frame rate, TV or color, which Ani and which extensions, which model, the age setting, and the reading-aloud and talking settings, with the date Talk to Ani was turned on), the story being told, the description being edited, the films made there, so they are still there after a reload, and the notebooks taught in Teach Ani, which leave the browser only when a student saves one as a file. API keys are kept only if Remember keys in this browser is ticked. No voice, recording or words heard are kept. Nothing is kept on a server. On shared computers, a Chrome guest window forgets everything when it closes; otherwise clear the site's data afterwards.

What leaves the computer. Nothing about the stories, unless someone:

  • uses Claude, ChatGPT or Gemini with a key: the story and Ani's instructions, or the words of Tell Ani what's wrong with the film as the glass box tells it, go from the page straight to that company, under its terms (Gemini Nano runs on the computer, and nothing leaves it);
  • uses Talk to Ani, once a grown-up has turned it on: what is said into a microphone goes to the browser's maker (Google for Chrome, Microsoft for Edge, Apple for Safari) to be turned into words, unless the browser does that on the computer, as the switch then says;
  • chooses Send this story to Ken, which the site offers for a story of one's own, where Ani stopped on it and in the Save panel: the page shows exactly what will be sent (the story, the description, the model, where Ani stopped, nothing about the person) and sends it only on Send it.

Loading the site fetches its fonts from Google Fonts. If something goes wrong in the page, a small notice asks: Ani ran into an error. Send a report to Ken, without any story, key or personal data? Nothing is sent, and nothing of Sentry's (the error-reporting service) is loaded, unless someone presses Send; what would be sent is a click away, and Don't ask again is kept in that browser.

Children. Ask them not to put real names, addresses or other personal details into stories, or into what they tell Ani is wrong. Model providers' terms have age limits and claude.ai accounts are for adults, so with children use Gemini Nano or your own key on your own computer.

Content. Ani's films are abstract shapes; in Ken's Cinderella the stepmother pushes and hits Cinderella, shown as shapes bumping. Language models can write unexpected things; the page shows the description in plain words before Ani films it.
